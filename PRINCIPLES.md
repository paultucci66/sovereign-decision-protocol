# Principles

The Sovereign Decision Protocol (SDP) is guided by a small set of principles.

These principles exist to keep the protocol simple, durable, and useful.

## 1. The Enterprise Standardized Data. It Never Standardized Judgment.

Organizations preserve transactions, documents, records, and state.

They rarely preserve the judgment behind consequential decisions in a computable form.

SDP exists to represent that judgment.

## 2. The Protocol Is the Medium of Exchange

SDP is not the product.

It is the medium of exchange for consequential decisions.

The monetized value belongs in Decision Control Plane implementations and other products that create, govern, store, analyze, and learn from Decision Objects.

The protocol should maximize adoption.

Products should create value.

## 3. Simplicity Enables Adoption

A protocol that tries to become the product limits its own adoption.

SDP should remain small.

It should define only what is required for independent systems to exchange consequential decisions.

## 4. Identity Is Not Authority

Authentication proves who someone is.

Authority determines what they may decide.

Identity providers should not be replaced by SDP.

They should be referenced by SDP.

## 5. Representation Is Not Execution

SDP records decisions.

It does not execute them.

Applications execute domain work.

Decision Control Planes orchestrate governance, authority, policy, context, evidence, review, memory, and learning.

SDP standardizes the Decision Object.

## 6. The Decision Is the Source

Enterprise systems own operational state.

The Decision Object preserves the authority, rationale, context, evidence, and intent behind consequential actions.

A transaction tells us what happened.

A decision explains why it happened.

Authority explains who was allowed to make it happen.

## 7. Knowledge May Inform. Policy Authorizes.

Knowledge can be retrieved, summarized, ranked, searched, and interpreted.

Policy must be exact, versioned, and attributable when used for enforcement.

A model may inform a decision.

It should not invent the authority for one.

## 8. Context Is Referenced, Not Embedded

Context and evidence may be large, distributed, confidential, or externally controlled.

SDP should record verifiable references to context and evidence rather than embedding full payloads by default.

Implementations may retrieve, store, summarize, redact, or reason over context.

The protocol records what mattered.

## 9. Materiality Is Contextual

Not every decision deserves the same ceremony.

A lunch choice is not material for most organizations.

A supplier choice may be material for a hospital, school district, restaurant chain, or defense contractor.

Organizations define materiality through policy.

SDP represents consequential decisions once they are within scope.

## 10. Classification Is a Pattern, Not a Mandate

Decision Objects may be public, internal, shared, restricted, compartmented, or subject to other disclosure rules.

SDP should support classification patterns without adopting any single government, military, legal, or enterprise classification regime as the universal standard.

The protocol records classification metadata.

The Decision Control Plane enforces access, disclosure, redaction, and audit.

## 11. Learning Requires Preserved Judgment

Organizations do not become learning organizations by collecting more data.

They become learning organizations by preserving judgment.

Decision Objects create the substrate for institutional memory, outcome comparison, drift detection, and organizational learning.

## 12. Standards Are Built Through Scrutiny

SDP should improve through challenge.

If a premise is wrong, it should be corrected.

If a field is unnecessary, it should be removed.

If an abstraction is too narrow, it should be generalized.

The goal is not to defend a conclusion.

The goal is to determine whether the conclusion follows from sound premises.
